PoR tries to answer that with a single number. Every season, a handful of hitters get talked about like they've made a leap that never actually happened. A .310 average, a career-high slugging percentage, a run of clutch hits in August — and by winter they're getting paid, or drafted, like a different player than the one who takes the field next spring. The reverse happens just as often: a hitter scorches the ball all year, runs into bad luck on balls in play, and gets written off. PoR — Progression or Regression — is a metric built to catch both of those hitters before the league catches up. It doesn't measure how good a player is. It measures how much of what happened to them was earned, and how much was noise. The core idea Baseball Savant already tracks "expected" versions of most outcome stats — xBA, xSLG, xwOBA — built from exit velocity and launch angle rather than what actually landed for a hit. When a player's real numbers run well ahead of their expected numbers, they've been getting help from balls finding grass. When real numbers run behind expected, they've been getting robbed. PoR takes that gap for four different stats — batting average, slugging, weighted on-base average, and BABIP — scales each to a 0–100 range, and averages them. The result is a single score: High PoR (60–100): this player got unlucky. Skill outpaced results. Low PoR (0–40): this player got lucky. Results outpaced skill. The full formula and scaling method are on the methodology page — this article focuses on whether it actually works. Does it actually predict anything? Across four seasons (2021–2024) and 1,423 qualified player-seasons, PoR score correlated with the following year's change in wRC+ at r = 0.47 — a real, moderate relationship, not a coin flip and not a certainty either. The signal gets much stronger once you add two filters that have nothing to do with luck: a plate-appearance minimum, and a prior-year wRC+ threshold. A hitter needs playing time for regression to the mean to actually show up, and an already-elite hitter has less room to "progress" than a mediocre one does — a ceiling effect that shows up clearly in the data. Filtered down to hitters who received at least 502 plate appearances the following season, and split by whether they were below- or above-average (wRC+) the year PoR was measured: High PoR, below-average hitters: improved 90.0% of the time (45 of 50) Low PoR, above-average hitters: declined 77.8% of the time (56 of 72) Tighten the wRC+ split further — below 90 instead of below 100, above 110 instead of above 100 — and the high-PoR accuracy climbs to 94.3%. Why BABIP earns a spot in the formula The first version of PoR used three inputs — BA, SLG, wOBA — all measured against their Baseball Savant "expected" counterpart. BABIP doesn't have an expected version, but it doesn't need one: league-average BABIP sits at almost exactly .300 every year. A hitter running well above .300 has been lucky on balls in play; well below, unlucky. Adding it as a fourth input, scored the same way as the other three, improved regression detection by 6–8 percentage points at every plate-appearance threshold tested, without meaningfully hurting progression accuracy. What PoR is not PoR is not a projection system, and it's not trying to be one. It doesn't output a projected batting line, and it doesn't know anything about age, health, swing changes, or role changes. It answers one narrower question: relative to how this player has actually performed, how much of that was batted-ball luck? That narrower scope is what makes it useful — it's a lens to point at a projection or a scouting report, not a replacement for either. Where this goes next The next step is testing PoR against established projection systems (Steamer, ZiPS) on the same player pool, to see whether it adds information those systems don't already have — particularly in the cases where PoR and a projection system disagree. That comparison, plus a running log of 2025 rookies flagged by PoR, will be added here as the work is finished.
