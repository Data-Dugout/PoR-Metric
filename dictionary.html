Dictionary of Terms

Plain-language glossary

Every stat and abbreviation used across this site, explained in plain English — no prior sabermetrics knowledge required.

PoR Progression or Regression
A 0–100 score that estimates how much of a hitter's season was driven by skill versus luck, and how likely they are to trend up or down the following year. 100 is top-tier progression (very unlucky, due to improve), 50 is roughly neutral, 0 is due for serious regression (very lucky, due to decline).
Expected Stats
A family of Statcast metrics that estimate what a hitter's numbers "should" be based on the quality of contact they made — exit velocity, launch angle, and sprint speed — rather than what actually happened once defense, ballpark dimensions, and shifts got involved. A 110 mph line drive that gets robbed by a diving catch is a .000 result, but its expected batting average might be .650, since that exact contact profile is a hit about 65% of the time.
xBA Expected Batting Average
The batting average a hitter's contact quality says they should have, based on the historical outcomes of similar batted balls.
xSLG Expected Slugging Percentage
Same idea as xBA, but for slugging — it also credits how hard and far the ball was hit, not just whether it fell in for a hit.
xwOBA Expected Weighted On-Base Average
The expected version of wOBA (see below) — a single number estimating overall offensive contribution based on contact quality alone.
wOBA Weighted On-Base Average
A rate stat that weighs every way of reaching base (walk, single, double, home run, etc.) by how much it actually contributes to scoring runs, rather than treating all hits equally the way batting average does.
BA − xBA
The gap between what a hitter actually batted and what their contact quality says they should have batted. A positive number means they hit better than their contact deserved (lucky); a negative number means they hit worse than their contact deserved (unlucky). Example: a .300 BA with a .250 xBA is a +.050 gap — a lucky season.
BABIP Batting Average on Balls in Play
Batting average counting only balls put in play (excludes strikeouts, walks, and home runs). League-average BABIP sits close to .300 almost every season, which makes it a useful quick gut-check for luck — a hitter running far above or below .300 on balls in play is usually due for some correction. Unlike the other three components in PoR, BABIP has no "expected" counterpart, so it's scaled directly against that season's range rather than compared to a projection.
MAX / RANGE
For each metric, MAX is the single biggest "luck" gap posted by any qualified hitter that season (i.e., however lucky the luckiest hitter in baseball was). RANGE is the distance between the unluckiest and luckiest hitter that year (MAX minus MIN) — the full spread of luck that happened across the league. Both are recalculated every season, which is why PoR scores aren't meant to be compared across different years.
wRC+ Weighted Runs Created Plus
A park- and league-adjusted offensive stat where 100 is exactly league-average; each point above or below 100 represents 1% better or worse than average. PoR uses wRC+ as a filter on the results (separating out already-elite hitters, who tend to improve less even when unlucky), not as an input to the score itself.
PA Plate Appearances
Every trip a hitter takes to the plate, including walks and sacrifices (a broader count than "at-bats"). PoR includes all players with 100+ PA in a given season as its qualification threshold.
Hard-Hit% / Barrel% / Exit Velocity
Contact-quality metrics that measure how hard and how well a ball was struck. These are useful context alongside a PoR score but are not folded into the PoR formula itself.
