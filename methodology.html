Methodology

How the score is built

The formula, the scaling, and the data behind every PoR score.

The formula

PoR is the average of four component scores, each comparing a hitter's actual outcome to their expected outcome (from Baseball Savant's batted-ball model), scaled against that season's full range of qualified hitters:

PoR = 14 [ MAXBA − (BA − xBA) RANGEBA + MAXSLG − (SLG − xSLG) RANGESLG + MAXwOBA − (wOBA − xwOBA) RANGEwOBA + MAXBABIP − BABIP RANGEBABIP ]
A high score means the player under-produced relative to their skill (unlucky, likely to progress). A low score means they over-produced (lucky, likely to regress).

What each part of the formula means

If you've never seen a formula like this before, here's what each piece is actually doing, using BA as the example (SLG and wOBA work the same way):

  • BA − xBA (Batting Average minus Expected Batting Average) — the gap between what a hitter actually batted and what their contact quality says they should have batted. A positive number means they hit better than their contact deserved — they got lucky. A negative number means they hit worse than their contact deserved — they got unlucky.

    Example: a hitter batting .300 with an xBA of .250 has a difference of +.050 — a lucky season, since the actual result outran what the quality of contact predicted.
  • MAXBA — the single biggest (most positive) BA−xBA gap recorded by any qualified hitter that season. In other words, however lucky the luckiest hitter in baseball was that year.
  • RANGEBA — the distance between that unluckiest hitter and the luckiest hitter that season (MAX minus MIN). It's the full spread of "luck" that actually happened across the league that year.
  • BABIP (Batting Average on Balls in Play) — has no "expected" counterpart, so it's treated differently: league-average BABIP sits close to .300 every season, so a hitter's raw BABIP is scaled directly against that year's min and max.

Putting it together: subtracting a hitter's own gap from that season's MAX, then dividing by the RANGE, turns their raw luck number into a clean 0–100 scale — the unluckiest hitter in baseball lands near 100, the luckiest lands near 0, and everyone else falls somewhere in between. Why wRC+ is a filter, not an input

An early version tried folding a hitter's prior-year wRC+ directly into the score — boosting PoR for below-average hitters and penalizing it for above-average ones. It made intuitive sense, but it made the metric worse: correlation with next-year wRC+ change dropped from 0.47 to 0.45. Elite hitters near their performance ceiling improve less even when unlucky, simply because they have less room to grow — so wRC+ works better as a filter applied after the score, not a weight baked into it.

Data sources

All batted-ball and expected-stat data comes from Baseball Savant's expected statistics leaderboard; all wRC+ values come from FanGraphs. Analysis covers qualified hitters (min. plate-appearance thresholds noted per test) across the 2021 through 2024 seasons, with each season's PoR score checked against that player's following-season performance.
