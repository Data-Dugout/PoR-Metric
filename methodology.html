The formula PoR is the average of four component scores, each scaled to a 0–100 range from that season's actual extremes: PoR = (BA100 + SLG100 + OBA100 + BABIP100) / 4 Each component compares a hitter's actual outcome to their expected outcome (from Baseball Savant's batted-ball model), then scales that gap against the full range seen across all qualified hitters that season: BA100 = ( MAXBA − (BA − xBA) ) / RANGEBA × 100 SLG100 = ( MAXSLG − (SLG − xSLG) ) / RANGESLG × 100 OBA100 = ( MAXwOBA − (wOBA − xwOBA) ) / RANGEwOBA × 100 BABIP100 = ( MAXBABIP − BABIP ) / RANGEBABIP × 100 BABIP has no "expected" counterpart, so it's treated differently: league-average BABIP sits close to .300 every season, so a hitter's raw BABIP is scaled directly against that year's min and max — no difference calculation needed. A high score means the player under-produced relative to their skill (unlucky, likely to progress). A low score means they over-produced (lucky, likely to regress). Why scale year-by-year Two scaling approaches were tested: a single fixed scale built from five years of league-wide data, and a scale rebuilt from each season's own min/max. Year-by-year scaling produced meaningfully better accuracy and larger usable sample sizes, at the cost of scores not being directly comparable across seasons (a PoR of 80 in 2022 isn't quite the same as an 80 in 2024). That trade-off was worth it — the metric is currently intended for within-season use, not for comparing a player's score across years. Year-specific scaling ranges used Year BA−xBA range SLG−xSLG range wOBA−xwOBA range BABIP range 2024 0.188 0.297 0.179 0.150–0.403 2023 0.148 0.366 0.182 0.194–0.440 2022 0.150 0.337 0.172 0.148–0.409 2021 0.146 0.279 0.157 0.170–0.456 Why wRC+ is a filter, not an input An early version tried folding a hitter's prior-year wRC+ directly into the score — boosting PoR for below-average hitters and penalizing it for above-average ones. It made intuitive sense, but it made the metric worse: correlation with next-year wRC+ change dropped from 0.47 to 0.45. Elite hitters near their performance ceiling improve less even when unlucky, simply because they have less room to grow — so wRC+ works better as a filter applied after the score, not a weight baked into it. Data sources All batted-ball and expected-stat data comes from Baseball Savant's expected statistics leaderboard; all wRC+ values come from FanGraphs.
